There are two oval cut diamonds on the table in front of you, two carats each. Both have identical colour, clarity, polish, and symmetry on their GIA certificates. One commands a significantly higher price than the other, and if you see them next to each other you will know why immediately.



No one really explains this part, so here goes.



A certificate is an inventory of a diamond's characteristics. It records what the stone weighs, how colourless it is, and how internally clean it is. These are useful facts, and a large stone should be bought with one. What most people do not know is that the grades on a certificate are not absolute. Weight and proportions are measured. Colour, clarity, and symmetry are judged, by a person, against a reference.



Start with colour. The scale runs from D to Z, and each grade is a band rather than a point. D and E sit next to each other and are often difficult to separate. The whole range is a gradual slide from colourless to light yellow, divided up for convenience.



Clarity works the same way, with more room inside each band. An SI1 can cover a wide gamut of inclusion type, size, and number. What matters is the nature of the inclusion and where it sits. An SI1 with everything hidden near the girdle can look better than a VS2 with a crystal under the table. The letters rank the inclusion. They do not rank your experience of it.



Then there is the gap most buyers never hear about. Cut is only graded on round brilliants. An oval, a pear, an emerald, a cushion, a marquise: none of them receive an overall cut grade from any major lab. They get polish and symmetry, which are different things and much narrower ones. So for every fancy shape, the single factor that decides how the diamond actually looks is the one thing the paper leaves blank. This is why two ovals with the same grades on paper can differ so completely, and why the market for fancy shapes rewards anyone who can judge them by eye.



Next come the proportions, printed on the certificate and easy to read, though nothing on the report interprets them. What it will not say is that a deep stone carries weight in its pavilion where you cannot see it, so a 1.00 carat can face up like a 0.90 while another faces up like a 1.15. You pay by the carat and you wear the face-up size. Reading those numbers well is most of the job.



Some things appear on no certificate at all. The bow-tie, that dark band across the centre of many ovals, pears, and marquises, is a proportion problem with no grade attached to it. You either look at the stone or you do not know.



And a certificate is a judgement made by people. Send the same stone to two labs, or the same lab twice, and it can come back a grade apart. Anyone who has traded stones for long enough has seen this. It is not scandal, it is just the nature of human grading at scale, and it is a reason to treat the paper as evidence rather than as the verdict.



None of this makes certificates worthless. We will not sell a large stone without one. It makes them a floor. The certificate tells you the diamond is what it is claimed to be. It does not tell you it is the best one for you, or that you are paying the right price for it.

\
That judgement is what four generations of sorting stones have taught us. We learned it the slow way, one parcel at a time, long before any of it was written down on a certificate. It is why, when you sit across from us with two stones in front of you, we can tell you what separates them and why one is worth more than the other. We look at the diamond first and the paper second, and we tell you what it really is.
